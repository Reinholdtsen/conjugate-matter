# The Universe's Other Half

### One broken symmetry as the common origin of dark matter, dark energy, and the fate of the cosmos

*What follows presents the Comatter framework as if it were confirmed. It is not. It is a speculative proposal awaiting expert review and observational tests, and it ends with the measurements that could refute it.*

---

## Two descriptions of one thing

Every sound admits two complete descriptions. A chord played on a piano can be recorded as pressure varying in time — the waveform — or as a list of frequencies and their strengths — the spectrum. Neither description is an approximation of the other. Each contains the whole sound, and a mathematical operation, the Fourier transform, converts either into the other exactly. Audio engineers move between the two views constantly, choosing whichever makes the task at hand simple.

Quantum mechanics says the same thing about matter. A particle's description in terms of position and its description in terms of momentum are two complete readings of one underlying state, related by the same Fourier transform that relates a waveform to its spectrum. And in the equations, the two readings have equal standing: exchange position for momentum throughout (with one sign adjustment) and the formalism is unchanged.

Max Born pointed this out in 1938 and asked the question it raises. If the two descriptions are equal in the mathematics, why is the world so unequal? Atoms are compact in position, not in momentum. Interactions occur between objects that are close together in space, never between objects that merely share a velocity. General relativity curves space and time and leaves momentum flat. Every known law that treats the two variables differently treats them differently in the same direction. Born found no answer, and the question has remained open since.

## An asymmetry that cannot be avoided

The Comatter framework starts from two observations. First, the asymmetry cannot reside in the basic laws, because the quantum kinematics demonstrably has the symmetry. Second, some asymmetry is unavoidable anyway. The uncertainty principle forbids anything from being sharply defined in position and momentum at once, so a universe containing definite structures — atoms, crystals, instruments — must be definite in one variable at the expense of the other. Perfect even-handedness is not available to any universe with things in it.

Physics has a standard name for a symmetry that is present in the laws but absent from the state: spontaneous symmetry breaking. The standard illustration is freezing. Liquid water is identical in every direction, and so are the laws governing its molecules. Freeze it, and the resulting crystal has definite axes. Nothing in the laws chose those axes; the transition had to settle on some orientation, and did. A careful observer living inside the ice would find preferred directions built into every material fact of its world, and could easily mistake them for preferred directions in the laws of nature.

The proposal is that the position–momentum symmetry is exact, and that the vacuum of our universe is the frozen state. In the earliest universe, the vacuum settled into a configuration organized around position. That configuration — not the underlying laws — is the reason interactions are local in space, the reason objects have places, and the reason momentum reads as bookkeeping rather than as an arena in its own right. The settling was compulsory, since the uncertainty principle allows no symmetric option; the particular outcome was not. A vacuum organized around momentum would have been equally lawful, in the way ice frozen along different axes is equally ice.

## The second geometry, and why it has never been seen

An exact symmetry between position and momentum requires the metric of spacetime — the field that specifies geometry and tells matter how to fall — to have a counterpart describing geometry in momentum space. When that counterpart is written down within the framework, it turns out to have the same character as the gravitational field itself: a second symmetric tensor field, in effect a second copy of gravity. That is a serious problem, because a theorem of field theory, proved in 2000, establishes that two interacting massless gravities are mathematically inconsistent. The symmetry appears to demand a forbidden theory.

The resolution operates like the Higgs mechanism. When the vacuum froze into its position-organized configuration, the second field absorbed part of the broken symmetry and acquired a large mass. A massive field mediates only short-range effects, so the second geometry is invisible to every experiment performed to date — while the familiar graviton remains exactly massless, in agreement with gravitational-wave observations. One mechanism accounts for both facts that needed explaining: the symmetry is genuine, and its second metric has never been detected, because the same transition that hid the symmetry made its gravity massive.

The mass mechanism has a further consequence. The symmetry constrains the massive field's interactions to a palindromic pattern — the same read forward and backward — and a short calculation shows that, under this pattern, the field's contribution to the energy density of empty space cancels exactly. The energy of the vacuum is a long-standing difficulty in physics: straightforward estimates exceed the measured value by some hundred and twenty orders of magnitude. Within this framework, the new field — which might have been expected to worsen the problem — contributes precisely zero. Whether that cancellation is new or a rediscovery of structure already implicit in the literature on related theories is an unresolved question; either way, it is a consequence of the symmetry, not an assumption fed in.

## Dark matter as the other polarization

Matter, like geometry, comes in both polarizations. Alongside ordinary matter — organized around position — the framework permits matter organized around momentum. Gravity couples to energy and is indifferent to polarization, so momentum-polarized matter falls, orbits, and bends light exactly as ordinary mass does. That is the complete observational profile of dark matter, assembled over ninety years: gravitational influence, and nothing else.

The distinguishing mark appears in its self-interactions, which operate only between particles whose velocities nearly coincide. The consequences match a pattern astronomers have struggled to engineer by hand. In the cold interiors of dwarf galaxies, where dark matter drifts at a few tens of kilometers per second, the interaction is active and smooths out the over-dense central cusps that simpler models predict but observations do not show. In galaxy-cluster collisions, at thousands of kilometers per second, the interaction shuts off and the dark matter passes through undisturbed — the behavior demanded by the Bullet Cluster. The signature is a knee: self-interaction strength dropping steeply at a characteristic velocity. The framework adds a diagnostic no one had previously asked the data for: the sharpness of that knee differs according to whether dark matter is a gas of particles or a coherent quantum wave, so the knee's measured shape identifies which it is.

As to what the momentum-polarized matter fundamentally is, the framework narrows the answer to three candidates, each with distinct observational handles: particles; a condensate of the massive geometric field itself, in which case dark matter and the hidden geometry are one substance; or topological solitons — stable configurations that cannot decay for the same reason a knot cannot untie, which would account, without further assumptions, for dark matter's perfect stability over thirteen billion years.

The newest layer of the framework sharpens the third candidate by joining it to a second symmetry. Electromagnetism has its own two-sided structure: electric and magnetic charge enter Maxwell's equations on formally equal footing, yet every particle ever detected carries the electric kind. Deciding which particles count as "electric" — fundamental, light, weakly coupled — and which count as "magnetic" — composite, heavy, strongly coupled — is a choice of description, and it is mathematically the same operation as deciding which variables count as position: the identical transformation generates both dualities. The framework's proposal is that the two divisions are one. The momentum-polarized sector is the magnetic side of electromagnetism, and its darkness has a quantifiable origin: a consistency condition derived by Dirac in 1931 fixes the product of the two kinds of charge, so the weakness of our electric coupling forces the magnetic coupling to be enormous — about 34, against our 1/137. Magnetic matter is therefore heavy, tightly bound, and solitonic: the natural population of knots.

Nor is the magnetic side expected to be a single species. A line of established theory anticipates a full magnetic counterpart for every electrically charged particle, quarks included: the proposal dates to 1977, and "magnetic quark" has been standard vocabulary in field theory since the 1990s, where exact dualities exchange a theory's electric particles for magnetic ones wholesale. The strongest form of the idea already serves as the leading explanation of a familiar mystery. No experiment has ever isolated a single quark, and the mechanism most widely credited — the dual superconductor picture — holds that the vacuum contains a condensate of magnetic charge that squeezes the force between quarks into an unbreakable string. If that picture is correct, magnetic analogs of quarks are not hypothetical inhabitants of a hidden sector; their condensed form is at work inside every proton. The framework's addition is the free spectrum: the momentum polarization carries the entire magnetic mirror-family — a matter sector as structured as our own, every member heavy and strongly bound by Dirac's condition, of which only the topologically protected knots survive the age of the universe as free relics.

A magnetically charged cosmos raises an immediate objection — should it not affect light? The answer is a standard piece of optics. Uniform media do not scatter light; they refract it. Glass interacts with every photon that crosses it and remains transparent, because scattering requires density fluctuations at the scale of a wavelength, and a smooth medium offers none. A dark sector of sparse, extremely heavy constituents is such a medium. Its induced refraction falls steeply with constituent mass, and at the masses natural for these solitons — around 10¹⁶ times the proton's, corresponding to roughly one object per twenty-five cubic kilometers of local space — the residual dispersion and polarization rotation of light lie some forty orders of magnitude below the sharpest astronomical measurements: the timing of radio bursts across billions of light-years, the polarization of the microwave background. Three independent requirements — thirteen-billion-year stability, the strong coupling Dirac's condition demands, and transparency to light — select the same corner of the three-way question. The question remains open, but its candidates are no longer equally placed.

## Dark energy as the orientation of the split

The frozen vacuum is characterized by an orientation: mathematically, an angle specifying how phase space is divided between the position side and the momentum side. In this framework, dark energy is the energy stored in that orientation's displacement from its resting value.

This identification carries obligations that a mere constant would not. The orientation is a physical variable, so its energy can change, and the framework fixes the direction of change. For dark energy to hold constant, the orientation would have to sit at an exact stationary value with exactly zero rate of change, which quantum fluctuations forbid, just as they forbid a particle from having exact position and exact stillness together. So the observed dark energy is a displacement, and a displaced quantity evolves toward rest: dark energy must decrease over cosmic time. It cannot do the opposite. Strengthening past the constant-energy line — the behavior cosmologists call phantom — would require the orientation's kinetic energy to be negative, and kinetic energy is the square of a rate of change; a square of a real quantity cannot be negative. Decreasing is required; phantom is impossible; constant is impossible.

Recent measurements bear directly on this. Galaxy surveys mapping tens of millions of objects have, over the past two years, reported statistically suggestive evidence that dark energy is weakening — the required behavior — though the case is not yet decisive. The next data releases, arriving over the next few years, will either strengthen the trend, flatten it back to a constant, or push it into the phantom regime. The framework survives only the first outcome.

The decline has an endpoint. The orientation's energy landscape dips below zero, and once the stored energy goes negative, the cosmic push becomes a pull: expansion slows, halts, and reverses. The momentum description makes the same statement in different words, and here the two descriptions of a sound become more than an analogy. Cosmic expansion stretches every wavelength — the redshift — which, read in the momentum description, means every momentum in the universe is shrinking: the spectrum of the cosmos has been contracting since the beginning. That is not a second process running alongside expansion; it is expansion, read in the other variable. The eventual reversal, likewise, is one event with two readings: in the position description, an expanding universe turning around; in the momentum description, a contracting one reaching minimum and rebounding.

## A second speed of light

In the momentum description, light propagates through momentum rather than position, and its invariant speed accordingly has the units of momentum per time — which is to say, the units of force. The framework's natural value for this limit is c⁴/G, roughly 10⁴⁴ newtons. A maximum force of exactly this form was conjectured within general relativity two decades ago, as the scale approached when black holes of comparable mass merge; in this framework the conjecture would acquire a reason, since a maximum force is simply the statement that nothing outruns light in the momentum description.

The rest of special relativity transcribes along with the speed limit. Forces combine the way velocities do: two forces applied together yield less than their arithmetic sum, by a correction that keeps the total below c⁴/G however the addition is iterated. Clocks subjected to sustained force run slow, by the same square-root formula that governs fast clocks, with force in place of velocity. Neither effect has ever been noticed, and the framework specifies why with a number: the strongest sustained force in accessible astrophysics — on a kilogram at a neutron star's surface, about 10¹² newtons — produces corrections of order 10⁻⁶⁵. Force has always appeared exactly additive for the same reason velocity appeared exactly additive before 1905: nothing at hand approaches the limit. The one natural process that does operate at the limit is the merger of comparable black holes, which is accordingly the one arena where corrections to ordinary kinematics could be of order unity.

The invariants exchange as well. In our description, the quantity all observers agree on is a particle's mass; in the momentum description it is a length — particles of the other polarization carry an unchangeable size as ours carry an unchangeable mass, realizing Born's original 1938 ambition of a physics with a fundamental mass and a fundamental length on equal footing. The two constants meet in a consistency check: a length multiplied by the maximum force is an energy, and the Planck length times c⁴/G is exactly the Planck energy. Rest exchanges too. To be at rest in the momentum description is to feel no force — which, in our description, is free fall. The momentum description's principle of relativity, that physics is identical in all unforced frames, is a statement already accepted under another name: the equivalence principle, the foundation Einstein chose for general relativity. In this framework his two relativity principles are one principle, read in the two descriptions. And one class of measurements does double duty: because light's energy–momentum relation traces the null cone of momentum space, every observation confirming that the speed of light does not depend on its energy has tested the rigidity of both cones at once.

Black holes then take on a second role. In the position description, a horizon bounds a region from which there is no spatial exit. In the momentum description, the same horizon is the surface where remaining stationary would require force beyond the limit. The framework also permits, though it has not yet constructed, the mirror-image object: a trapped region in momentum rather than position, from which no attainable force can extract a captive. Such an object would be opaque in the momentum description and transparent in ours — undetectable by light, evident only through its gravity, and therefore indistinguishable, to our instruments, from compact dark matter.

## The other observers

If the momentum-polarized sector includes complex structures — a possibility the framework permits but does not require — observers there would perceive the world through momentum rather than position. Their sky would be a map of velocities. The orbital motions of our galaxies would register directly in their observations, while the galaxies' locations would not. Their astronomers would infer a large unseen influence organized in a variable their instruments cannot resolve, and would be correct. Each polarization is the other's dark sector.

## What one assumption purchases

The framework rests on a single assumption — the position–momentum symmetry is exact and spontaneously broken — and the accounting runs as follows. The primacy of space over momentum: a property of the frozen vacuum, not of the laws. The century-long invisibility of the symmetry: the mass of the second gravitational field. The quietness of empty space, at least as concerns that field: the palindromic cancellation. Dark matter: the other polarization of matter, marked by a velocity-gated interaction with a measurable knee. Dark energy: the vacuum orientation's displacement, obligated to decrease. A candidate origin for the conjectured maximum force, carrying the rest of special relativity with it — including the equivalence principle as the other description's principle of relativity. A candidate identity for dark matter's charge: the magnetic half of electromagnetism's own duality, with the choice of electric and the choice of position as a single operation performed twice. And a revised ending for the universe: not indefinite expansion into cold, but an eventual turnaround — equivalently, in the other description, a contraction reaching bottom and rebounding.

Whether any of this is true is a question with a schedule. The framework forbids a constant dark energy and forbids a phantom one, and the surveys now measuring dark energy's history will report, within a few years, whether those prohibitions hold.
